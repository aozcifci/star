## PROLOGUE

### Applause

*January 9, 2019 — New Orleans*

The studio lights were hot. Tobias Usher was not sweating.

The red eye of camera forty-nine watched him, and he looked not at the camera but at the darkness just above it, as if there, beyond the reach of the lights, sat a congregation only he could see. A Bible lay on his knees, bound in black, its cover worn thin in one place from years of the same hand holding it the same way.

"Mr. Usher," said Verna Cole. Hers was the voice that had entered America's living rooms every noon for eleven years: warm, patient, never in a hurry. "When did you last see your sister-in-law Miriam?"

"Ten years ago," Toby said. "It was December. She was standing by the car. She was cold. I gave her my coat."

A lie of this quality is like a well-polished table. It reflects the light. A man leans over it and sees his own face, and believes his own face.

"You gave her your coat," Verna said.

"I did. I have only ever served that family, Ms. Cole. God is my witness." He stroked the cover of the Bible the way a man strokes a cat — certain, proprietary. "She disappeared that night. I have prayed for her every night for ten years."

Three hundred people sat in the studio, leaning forward. Across the rest of the country, millions had forgotten their lunches and were staring at the same calm face. They had been staring at it for three weeks. For three weeks this man had come to every broadcast, answered every question with the same softness, and with every answer the absence of a woman and a child had grown a little larger.

Verna Cole heard the small receiver in her ear crackle. The line producer's voice came through from the control room. Two words.

*"They're coming."*

Verna looked at the cards in her hand. The next question was written there: *When were you last at Salt Point?* She would never ask it. If eleven years in this business had taught her one thing, it was this: some questions are not asked. They are waited for.

"Mr. Usher," she said. "Would you like some water?"

Toby smiled. His teeth were even. "I don't thirst," he said. "The flesh thirsts. The spirit doesn't."

That was when the doors opened.

There were eight of them. The navy jackets of the state police looked almost black under the studio lights. The one in front glanced once at the paper in his hand and twice at the man in the chair. Somewhere in the audience a woman gave a short, strangled cry — from excitement or from fear, no one could say, not even the woman.

"Tobias Usher." The trooper's voice carried without a microphone; the silence of the studio delivered it to every seat. "Stand up, please."

Toby did not stand. First he set the Bible on the side table. Carefully. He squared its corner with the corner of the table. Then he rose and held out his hands, as though he were directing this scene too, as though the handcuffs had been his idea.

"Verna," he said, using her first name for the first time. The cameras were rolling. "This is a trial. Let the viewers know. This is a trial, and I am ready."

The handcuffs closed with a dry click. Like the snapping of a small bone.

And then — Verna Cole would carry this to the end of her life — the applause began. One person first, then ten, then three hundred. They rose to their feet. They clapped. Some of them were crying. Some had their phones out, filming. The troopers walked Toby toward the doors and the applause followed him all the way, like a wedding procession, like a funeral, like both at once.

Verna did not cut away. She could have; one raised finger and the control room would have gone to commercial. She did not. She stood and watched, and a voice inside her — a voice she would never confess to at any dinner party for the rest of her days — said: *This is the highest rating of your life. And it happened because a woman is dead.*

---

Three states away, in Tennessee, in room fourteen of a motel by the highway, a nineteen-year-old boy sat on the end of a bed.

The television was an old model, bolted to a shelf near the ceiling, and the boy had been looking up at it until his neck ached. For three weeks, every day at noon, he had left the housekeeping cart in the corridor, come into this room, and turned on this channel. He worked at the motel. Nobody asked him anything. He had come here so that nobody would ask him anything.

On the screen, his uncle was being handcuffed.

Robbie Hale did not move. He had expected joy of himself; for years he had built this scene, in the blank minutes before sleep, against bus windows, over sinks of dishwater. The joy did not come. What came from the screen was applause, three hundred people's applause, and inside it Robbie heard only one thing: a woman's name. No one was saying it. He heard it anyway.

*Miriam.*

It was his mother's name.

Robbie stood, switched off the television, looked for a moment at his own reflection in the dead screen — his mother's chin, his mother's brows — then went out to the corridor and pushed the cart down to room fifteen. He changed the sheets. He wiped the mirror. His hands did not shake. His hands never shook. He knew exactly who had taught them that.

That night he stood by the ice machine behind the motel and looked at the dark for a long time, and for the first time in ten years he said it out loud — to no one, to the night:

"I know where you are."

Then he went inside, lay down, and did not sleep.

In the morning he walked to the bus station and bought a ticket south. Toward home. Toward Louisiana.
