# PART FOUR — MISSING

*December 2018 – January 2019*

---

*Ten years, and it was a studio light that finally spelled your name.*

*Not the law. Not the church. Not the town with its casseroles and its averted porches. A television program between a car-insurance commercial and a cooking segment — that is what the world sent, in the end, for you and your daughter. You would have laughed. You had that laugh you kept for the world's timing, the one your son inherited without ever hearing it.*

*But understand this, before you judge the vessel: the truth is water. It does not care what carries it. A courtroom, a confession, a game show — water takes the shape of whatever finally holds it.*

*And listen — someone is knocking at a lawyer's door. It is your little brother, the reader, the one who said nothing for twelve years and burned all twelve years for fuel.*

*Watch what the quiet ones do, Miriam. You always said it. Watch the quiet ones.*

## 19

### The Quiet One

Victor Weaver had rehearsed his opening sentence for four months, and when he finally sat down in the office between the tax preparer and the nail salon — the same strip mall; the town was small and its geometry unforgiving — what came out instead was:

"How much do you charge to reopen a grave?"

Dale Prejean, attorney-at-law, forty-one years old, a man whose practice ran to fender disputes and fishing-boat liens, looked across his desk at the visitor: mid-thirties, glasses, a dry mouth on him, hands flat on his knees like a man in a crash brace. Prejean had grown up in this parish. He knew who the Weavers were. Everyone knew who the Weavers were, the way everyone knows where the sinkhole is.

"Depends whose grave," he said.

"Nobody knows whose," Victor said. "That's the problem I'm bringing you." And then he took off his glasses, and rubbed the bridge of his nose, and began — in the flat, over-ordered voice of a man who has kept a thing in a pressure vessel for a decade — to empty the inventory. His family's native gift, it turned out. It ran in the blood alongside the silence, waiting for it to fail.

He talked for two hours. Prejean stopped taking notes after four minutes, because a lawyer's notepad has margins and this did not. The tree. The lantern. The ledger with the mallard. Sixteen struck days. The pines west of Ferris Creek. The nephew who had gone over the fence twice, whereabouts unknown, God keep him. The other boy, Ethan, *taken by masked men* — Victor did the quotation marks with his voice, and in them, for just a second, Prejean heard twelve years of supper-table performances, and his skin walked.

"Why now?" Prejean asked, when it was down. It was the only lawyer question that mattered. Juries would ask it; the town would ask it; the internet, God help them all, would ask it in capital letters. *Twelve years at that table. Why now?*

Victor looked out the window at the parking lot for a while.

"My father gets out in the spring," he said. "He went in a hero. Five years became eight became eleven; you know how that arithmetic goes for a man who won't say sorry for a thing he didn't do. All those years, visiting days, I watched him get older through the glass and I thought: he doesn't know. He still thinks Miriam ran off west. He still thinks Mellie's with cousins in Texas. He's coming home in April to a house with holes puttied over and he is going to start asking the questions we buried, one by one, out loud, at that table." He put his glasses back on. "Mr. Prejean, my brother-in-law has answered every hard question in that family for twelve years, and the people who asked them have a way of being called away. I am not here for a grave. I'm lying to you already. I'm here because my father is the bravest man I know and he's about to walk through that door blind, and I—" the pressure vessel went, all at once, undramatically, the way they do; he cried like a man reading numbers off a page — "I said nothing. Twelve years, I said *nothing*. You put that anywhere you want in the file."

Prejean got him a glass of water. Then he did what small-town lawyers do, which is never what the movies think: he laid out, patiently, the geometry of the impossible. The file was closed — he'd pulled the docket while Victor talked; INactive, 2014, leads exhausted. A closed file needs new evidence to reopen; a recanted statement is anti-evidence, it inoculates; an in-law's twelve-year-old hearsay was a motion to dismiss that wrote itself. The DA golfed — still, yet, always. Physical evidence meant digging on private land, which meant a warrant, which meant probable cause, which meant a witness on the record, which meant one of six people who had spent twelve years demonstrating, under professional supervision, exactly how good they were at recanting.

"Get me one of them on the record before your brother-in-law knows the wind's changed," Prejean said, "and I'll die on this for you. But the moment — the *moment* — a detective calls that house, they'll fold back into one animal. They've had twelve years of drill. I can't take six depositions simultaneously, son. Nobody can."

And then he stopped.

Because he had heard himself say *simultaneously*, and through the wall of his office, faint but eternal, came the sound of his receptionist's daytime television.

Victor watched the lawyer's face change and would describe it, years later, to a documentary camera, as *the face of a man doing something between remembering and repenting.*

"My aunt Dot," Prejean said slowly, "held a boy's hand in the harbor office in 2010 and never got over what the machine did with it. She calls me every Christmas and asks if the law's grown a spine yet." He turned his chair to face the wall the television was behind, as if he could see it. "There's a woman on Channel 4, middle of the day. Verna Cole. My receptionist plans her lunch around her. She finds missing folks live on the air — she sits the whole family on one couch, cameras rolling, cops on the tip line, ten million housewives taking notes. I used to think it was carrion TV." He turned back. "Mr. Weaver. What if all six of them answered questions at the same time, in public, on the record, in front of the entire United States — and thought it was their idea?"

The room was quiet.

"He'd never allow it," Victor said. "He controls every—" and then he stopped too, because twelve years at that table had taught him the other thing, the thing he'd watched work on his own mother:

You did not get Toby to do something by allowing it.

You got Toby to do something by making it the biggest stage in the room.

## 20

### The Application

*Verna.*

Eleven years I'd been doing the show, and I want this understood before anything else, because everything I say after it is compromised: I was good at it, I believed in it, and I knew exactly what it was. All three at once. If you can't hold three things at once, you've never worked in television.

*MISSING* found people. That was true. One hundred and forty-one people in eleven years; I kept the number the way Robbie Hale, though I didn't know him yet, kept a Band-Aid tin. It was also a noon show with a jingle, sold against detergent, and its beating heart was a couch where American families sat in their church clothes and came apart in high definition. Both things. At once. My executive producer called it *the ministry* with a straight face, and called it *the property* on the same phone call.

The Weaver application came in through the website on the third of December, 2018, filled out — this mattered enormously later, and I've testified to it twice — by the family itself. Sofia flagged it in the Tuesday meeting. Missing woman, missing child, cold since '08 and '09, out of a parish I'd have to look up. On paper, honestly: thin. A decade cold, no bodies, no suspect, family the complainants. I passed. God's truth, I passed on it, and it was Sofia — twenty-six, my researcher, hungry the way I'd stopped letting myself be — who slid her laptop across the table and said, "Look at the application again. Look who filled which part."

The form had a field: RELATIONSHIP TO THE MISSING. Six family members were listed as willing to appear. Six. Families of the missing beg to appear alone; getting a second relative on that couch is like pulling teeth out of a horse. Six of them, wanting to come together — *insisting*, per the follow-up call, on coming together — and at the bottom, under WHO REFERRED YOU TO US, in the careful handwriting of, we later established, Emma Usher, taking dictation:

*"Our brother-in-law, a man of God, says it is time the country helped us look. He watches your program daily and says you have an honest face."*

"He's the applicant," Sofia said. "Whoever this brother-in-law is — the missing woman's file is his idea. He's inviting us in. Verna, in eleven years, has the couch ever come to *us*?"

I have been asked a thousand times — by lawyers, by columnists, by a Senate staffer once, at a party, ghoulishly — when I first knew. The honest answer is disliked in every one of those rooms, so here it is for the record instead. I knew nothing. I suspected nothing. What happened was smaller and worse: I read that sentence, *he says you have an honest face*, and I felt the specific cold you feel when something in a dark aquarium turns out to be looking back at you. And then I looked at the overnight numbers from November, which were soft, and I heard myself say:

"Book them for the nineteenth. All six. One couch."

Both things. At once. You want a heroine, the library's the next building over.

What I did not know — what none of us knew until it was all four thousand pages of transcript — was that our website had been left open on the tall house's one sanctioned computer for nine days before that application was filled in. Left open by Victor Weaver, who had said at supper, mildly, that some show back East had found a Baton Rouge man's sister after nineteen years, imagine that; who had then gone to bed, and let the property sit in the room with the man who could not resist a stage the size of a country. Nine days. The spider believed to his last day in that studio that the web was his.

I've met a lot of clever men in my business. The cleverest ones are all owned by the same blind spot, and Victor Weaver, a community-college quiet man with a library card, had found it without a single course in psychology: a man who believes he is the smartest person in every room will walk through any door on which someone has written, in letters only he can read, *biggest room*.

They arrived on the nineteenth of December in one van. Church clothes. A hard, close bloc in my green room, answering in one voice, deferring with their eyes to the soft-spoken man who sat, I noticed, nearest the door — I notice seating; seating is my trade — with a black Bible worn thin in one place, and who rose when I entered, and took my hand in both of his, warm, dry, unhurried, and looked at me the way honest faces get looked at by whatever lives in the dark part of the aquarium.

"Miss Cole," said Tobias Usher. "We prayed on you. We were told you'd be the one."

Fourteen days later I watched the state police walk him out of my studio to a standing ovation, and I have spent every year since learning to hold the fourth thing, the one nobody tells you about, the one that doesn't fit in a hand with the other three:

We were both right.

## 21

### Sweeps

*Verna.*

Here's what daytime does that no courtroom can: it repeats.

A trial gets one telling from each witness — objections trimming it, a judge pacing it. My couch got six tellings a day, five days a week, in front of ten million line editors with laundry going, and the American housewife, I will put this on my tombstone, is the finest forensic instrument this country has ever declined to fund. By the third broadcast the tip line wasn't giving us leads. It was giving us *discrepancies*, time-stamped, cross-referenced, some of them in Excel.

Broadcast one, the family told it as rehearsed — I could not prove it was rehearsed, I could only hear it, eleven years of couch tells you when grief has stage directions. Miriam, troubled since her husband's death, wandered from a *campsite* in a storm. The child, sent to *cousins in Texas* the following year for her nerves, name and town gone vague with the years. On the couch's far end the brother-in-law held the hard bloc's center of gravity without touching a single question — until the last ninety seconds, when he asked, so humbly, whether he might say a word to the country, and thanked *the honest viewers of this program* for lending their eyes to a broken family, and quoted Matthew, and cried one tear. One. Camera two got it. The overnights came in and the property had its best December number in four years, and Sofia put a printout on my desk with a sticky note that said, in her small handwriting: *he watched the monitor, not you. he was checking his frame.*

Broadcast four, the discrepancies started walking on their own. A tip-line caller — retired teller, Gulf Federal, Prosperity Street — remembered a December loan, a close-knit family, a look held one beat too long; my producers found the paper. On air I asked, gently, who had co-signed for a troubled woman eight days before she vanished. The couch reorganized itself — you can watch it on the clips, everyone shifting a quarter-inch toward the man by the door, iron filings and a magnet — and Toby answered, sorrowfully, at length, and beautifully, about a family lifting a widow toward independence, and it played, it played fine, and that night Sofia's sticky note said: *ask short questions. his answers only work at length. clip him to eight seconds and he sounds like what he is.*

I started asking short questions.

Broadcast seven, we aired the substation. God forgive me the staging of it: the poster-size statement, the red circle on 11:41 p.m., the slow pan. *She stated "I am the complaint. Look at me."* I read it aloud, all of it, in the flat voice I save for things that need no help, and then I turned to Evie Weaver, seventy-one years old now, hands folded on her church dress, and I asked her the shortest question of my career.

"Ma'am. Did you write this?"

And I watched a woman deny her own nine miles of rain. Deny the house shoes. Deny the blanket she'd folded square. Medications, sleepwalking, grief, her nerves, so much trouble for everyone, so ashamed — the recantation of 2011 performed live and letter-perfect *seven years later*, and that, that fluency, was the moment the country's stomach turned over, because you cannot fake the sound of a lesson that has been drilled. Ten million people heard a woman recite. The clip did forty million views by Sunday under a caption I won't dignify, and three separate psychologists went on cable to say the words *coercive control*, and the *Times-Picayune*, which had never once, in ten years, sent a stringer to Mercer Parish for a missing mother and child, ran twenty-six hundred words titled THE FAMILY THAT ANSWERS TOGETHER.

And Fontenot called the tip line.

Not with a tip. Detectives don't tip. She called — she has told it herself, publicly, at cost to herself, so I'm free to tell it too — to ask one question of my senior producer, off the record, cop-flat: *is the network's evidentiary chain on that statement clean enough to survive a warrant application, yes or no.* Because down in Mercer Parish, a DA who golfed had spent seven days discovering that ten million voters were watching him not act on a case his office had boxed in 2014, and the annex had been asked, that morning, to locate 09-0004, and somewhere on Route 27 a records clerk was, at that hour, carrying toward a desk a bankers box with a church-dress photograph clipped inside the lid.

Mail, arriving. Postage due.

The seventh broadcast ended, the couch filed out, and in the corridor — this is in no transcript; this is mine — Tobias Usher stopped in front of me on his way to the van, close, warm, unhurried, Bible in the crook of his arm like a stunned bird, and said, "Short questions this week, Miss Cole. The Enemy loves brevity. He hasn't the patience for truth." And he smiled at me with those even teeth, and I looked into the middle of the sorrowful face, into the flat interested part, and I understood three things in one drop of studio time.

He still believed the web was his.

Something with better handwriting than mine was tightening around him broadcast by broadcast.

And I was going to keep booking him until it closed — for the hundred and forty-one, I'd tell the Senate staffer at the party; for the number, I'd tell the dark at four a.m. Both things.

At once.

## 22

### The Green Room

*Robbie.*

The bus from Tennessee takes eleven hours and I stood the last two, because sitting still had stopped being a thing my body agreed to do somewhere south of Jackson.

I want to be exact here. This part gets told wrong in every account, and it's mine, so: I did not come to be on television. I had watched seven broadcasts from room fourteen by then, and I had seen what the couch did to the ones who sat on it, and I knew the couch was the lawyer's move and the detective's move and the country's move, and it was working, and none of it needed me. What needed me was a different arithmetic, the original one, the one from the wagon's middle seat, and it had kept me awake three nights running until I finally said it out loud to the ice machine:

Ethan got the postcard. Ethan went over the fence on a Tuesday. Which means Ethan is twenty-two somewhere, watching the same channel I am, alone in whatever room he cleans or kitchen he works, watching his father's even teeth every noon, waiting to see if the fence held. If nobody stands up in the light where he can see them, he stays over the fence forever. Fences keep you out of places too. Nobody tells kids that part.

So I came to stand in the light. That's all. Put it on the record plain.

Sofia met me at the security desk. I'd emailed the tip line four words and a photograph of a Band-Aid tin — *I'm the nephew. Robbie.* — and I'll say this for Verna Cole's people: nobody made me say the inventory twice. I said it once, in a conference room, all of it, in order, the flat voice, the whole tin — first time whole since a dock in 2010 — and when I finished, Verna Cole, who I'd only ever seen at a hard up-angle from a motel bed, sat with her knuckle against her mouth for a long time and then said the only thing anyone in that building ever said that made me trust them:

"I can't put you on that couch, Robbie. He'd spend the hour farming you, and the country would watch him do it, and it'd rate a nine." She slid the conference phone across. "What I can do is dial the Mercer Parish detective who's been waiting ten years for a witness who never learned to recant. You never learned, did you?"

"No, ma'am," I said. "I skipped those classes."

We were forty minutes into the call with Fontenot — her voice on speaker exactly like I'd imagined a level sounds if you make a level talk — when it happened. What happened is: green rooms share a corridor with everything, and vans run late, and a researcher opened our door looking for Sofia at the same moment the far door opened for the family bloc coming in for broadcast eight, and for one clean second, down forty feet of hallway carpet the color of oatmeal, I was looking at them.

All of them. Older, smaller, church-clothed. My grandmother, hands folded, eyes down. Angela Mae with her arm in Emma's. Jesse behind — Jesse who cried at a Trailways counter and then went home and tied the knots — gray now, stooped like a question mark, hands doing their thing against his coat.

And at the door, nearest the door, always nearest the door: the coat-adjuster. The table-squarer. The warm heavy hand.

Ten years I'd built what I'd do with one clean second. Shout, or cross the carpet, or hold up the tin like a badge, like evidence, like a heart. I did none of it. I stood in a doorway in the light and I let him see me.

He stopped. The bloc bunched behind him like train cars.

And I watched the face do what I knew it would do, what I'd filed at ten years old over a breakfast of crackers — I watched it *practice*. Grief, half a beat late. Wonder, warming. The mouth opening on some psalm about the prodigal, arms beginning to rise for the country's benefit even though there were no cameras in that hallway, because by then the man rehearsed even for an audience of oatmeal carpet—

But the eyes. The eyes went where they always went before the play started, the flat interested place, the appraisal, the arithmetic — *what does he cost, what does he bring, whose is he* — and I stood there and let him finish the math and watched it come up, for the first time in his life with me, short.

Because I wasn't his. I was the auditor.

Nobody spoke. Sofia's hand closed on my elbow. The far door swallowed the bloc, him last, unhurried, and the second was over, and I went back into the green room and sat down and put my hands flat on the table, and on the speakerphone the level said, patient, like it had heard the whole thing in my breathing:

"Robbie. You still with me? I need the pines, son. West of Ferris Creek. Can you take me to the pines?"

My hands did not shake. You know who taught them that.

"Yes, ma'am," I said. "I've been carrying the map ten years."

## 23

### Five Days

*Verna.*

On the eighth of January, a Tuesday, my show received a lesson in what it was, and the country received a lesson in what it had been watching, and Tobias Usher received five days in the parish jail, and only one of the three of us understood the sentence correctly at the time.

It surfaced sideways, the way real things always came to us — not through the tip line but through a courthouse stringer doing a routine warrant sweep: *Usher, Tobias — active bench warrant, violation of protective order, 2016.* Buried paper, two years old. In 2016 a psychologist at a children's clinic two parishes over had examined the Usher children — there were two more by then; I want that fact in the record and I want it to sit there and be looked at — and had found what she found, and had done her mandated duty, and a family court had issued a protective order that Tobias Usher had violated within six weeks, and the machine had issued its warrant, and the warrant had sat. For two years. While the man it named ate lunch on national television.

We verified it by four p.m. By six I had the executive producer in my office doing the arithmetic of the property out loud — *if he's arrested before Thursday we lose the couch, the couch is the number, Verna* — and I threw him out, and I'm proud of that, and let the record also show it cost me nothing, because I already knew what the number would do either way. Both things. Always both things.

They took him at the studio door Wednesday morning, quietly, two deputies, no cameras — his choreography for once not his own — and the bloc on the sidewalk did something I will spend my retirement failing to describe on the first try: it *wavered*. Not toward him. Around him, like water around a piling when the tide turns — Angela Mae's arm coming out of Emma's, Jesse's spine unbending one vertebra, my producer swears he saw Evie Weaver's chin come up off her chest for the length of one breath — the first tide-turn in that family in twelve years, live on a municipal sidewalk, and then he said, gently, over his shoulder, being folded into the cruiser: *"Fast until Sunday. I'll be back before the fast is broken,"* and the water closed back over the piling, and they fasted.

He was back in five days. A protective-order violation, time served, docket cleared; the machine that had slept for two years apologized for the inconvenience with its whole institutional body. And on Monday he walked back into my studio — the lawyers upstairs having concluded, in a memo I framed, that un-booking him now posed *greater legal exposure than booking him* — and he sat down on my couch in front of eleven million people, eleven now, the number fed by its own scandal, and he folded his hands and said:

"They put me with thieves for five days, Miss Cole. Paul got the same lodgings. I sang."

And eleven million people watched him shine, and some percentage of them — this is the part of my trade I have never made peace with, the part I went off and testified to a Senate subcommittee about after I retired, unpaid, twice — some percentage of them, in eleven million living rooms, *believed him*. The mail came in sacks. Marriage proposals. Checks, actual checks, for his *ministry*. A man two years into an active warrant for what was in that clinic file, and my couch had done what no pulpit in his life ever did for him: it had made him national. Sofia quit that Friday. Her sticky note is also framed: *the couch works both ways. you knew that. so did he.*

But here is what he did not know, singing in the parish jail those five days, certain as arithmetic that the fast would hold the bloc:

Fasts break from the inside.

On the third day of it — hungry, unsupervised, unpreached-at for the first three consecutive days in twelve years — Angela Mae Weaver, forty now, migraines gone silver at her temples, walked on her own two feet into the office between the tax preparer and the nail salon, sat down across from Dale Prejean, put her church purse on her knees, and said:

"My brother Victor says you're the man keeping a list of what really happened. I'd like to add to it. I held her arm, Mr. Prejean. The night at the tree, and other nights. I held my own sister's arm." Her hands were flat and still on the purse. "The Enemy hasn't the patience for truth. Well. I've gotten patient."

Deposed, notarized, sealed: one of six, on the record, before the wind changed. The geometry of the impossible, broken by a jailhouse fast and a woman nobody had ever once asked the time of day.

Fontenot had her warrant application on a judge's desk — a different judge; the state had sent one down who golfed with no one — by Thursday.

He was singing on my couch on Monday.

The doors opened Wednesday.

## 24

### The Night It Broke

*Verna.*

Tuesday night, the night before the doors, we did the broadcast nobody planned, least of all me.

It was to be a holding pattern — I knew about the warrant application; I knew nothing of its timing; Fontenot ran her machine dark and was right to — so the rundown said: recap, tip-line montage, couch. Eleven million watching a rerun with living furniture. And then, four minutes in, during the montage — clips of three weeks of tellings, the campsite, the storm, the cousins in Texas, playing back to the very people who had told them, on the studio wall, nine feet tall—

Jesse Weaver started talking.

Not to me. That was what stopped the room; the floor manager's arm froze mid-cue. He was looking up at the wall, at his own gray face nine feet tall saying *she wandered off, storms confuse her*, and from the middle of the couch, in the voice of a man reading numbers off a page, Jesse Weaver said:

"That's not true. What he's saying there. That's not a true thing."

I did not move. Eleven years of couch — you learn the physics of a breaking dam: you do not gesture at the water. Camera three found him on its own; that boy on camera three should be teaching at a film school.

"Jess," said Toby. One syllable. Warm. The hand starting toward the shoulder, the old physics, iron filings—

"I only held the rope."

Five words, and the studio's air changed pressure; you can hear it on the master tape, a sound like the building inhaling, three hundred people's worth. Jesse's face had come apart in some way that had nothing to do with crying, the way a knot comes apart, fiber by fiber, twelve years of load releasing, and he was still looking up at the wall, at himself, and it poured out of him at that same terrible flat pitch: the rope, the nights, the cup — *Mama carried the cup, Mama, tell them about the cup* — and Evie Weaver's hands rose off her church dress and hung in the air holding an enamel cup that wasn't there, that hadn't been there for ten years, and eleven million people watched an old woman's hands remember what her mouth had spent a decade denying, and Angela Mae said, clearly, into her sister's shoulder, "It's done, Mama. I already told the list man. It's done," and Emma stood up and sat down and stood up again like a woman in a burning house—

And Tobias Usher rose from my couch.

Unhurried. Even then, unhurried. He buttoned his jacket, and he turned to camera one — not to the couch, never once again to the couch; he had audited it in half a second and moved the assets — and he raised the black Bible with the worn place, and he preached. Two minutes and forty seconds, unbroken, over Jesse's flat pouring voice, over Angela Mae, over the studio's held breath: the Enemy, the fire, the sifting of houses, *whom the Lord loveth He chasteneth* — magnificent, by any technical measure the finest television of my life, and I stood there in my own lights letting it run long past every line where I should have cut, and I have sworn to a subcommittee and I will swear here: not for the number.

Because down in the dark below camera one, where he couldn't see it and I could, the stage manager was holding up the call sheet for tomorrow with one line on it, and the line was blank, the way we mark it when law enforcement has told us to hold a slot and won't say for what.

And behind the sermon, at the edge of frame, in the corner of the master tape where eleven million people weren't looking, you can see me look down at that blank line, and then up at the man mid-verse, and you can see — I've watched it; everyone has watched it; it has been watched forty million times under a caption I did not choose but cannot argue with — you can see me decide to let him finish.

*Let the country have the whole sample,* the caption says I was thinking.

What I was thinking was simpler and I'll write it down at last, one hollow to another:

*Sing, preacher. It's the last set. The tide chart is real.*

## 25

### Applause (Reprise)

*Verna.*

The doors opened at 12:41.

You have seen it. Everyone alive that year has seen it; it plays somewhere on earth every hour, the way certain footage never stops falling. So let me give you only what the forty million views don't hold — what it was like to be standing in my mark, nine feet from the man, when the play finally, finally lost its stage.

He set the Bible down and squared its corner with the table's corner. You've all seen that. What the wide shot doesn't carry: the sound he made doing it. A small exhale through the nose — bookkeeping, a ledger closed at end of day. Whatever the psychologists have said since, on however many panels: no fear in it. I was nine feet away. The last accounting he did as a free man was of a prop's right angle, and it balanced, and that satisfied him.

Then the applause. Three hundred up on their feet, the phones, the crying, the wedding-funeral of it, and my finger not lifting for the cut, and the voice saying *highest rating of your life, and it happened because a woman is dead* — all of it exactly as I've set down in the beginning, because I began this testimony where my part of the guilt begins and I don't get to revise it on the second pass.

But standing inside the applause, I saw the two things the cameras missed, and they are the reason I have written any of this at all.

The first: the couch. In the widest shot it's a blur behind the arrest, but I was facing it. Five people sat in the storm of that ovation, and not one of them was looking at the man being taken. Jesse looked at his hands, freed of their rope at last and no idea what hands are for. Emma looked at the doors long after they'd closed, a widow at a rail. Angela Mae looked at me — level, spent, the patient religion — and gave me one small nod I have not earned yet and am still working off. And Evie Weaver, in the middle, in the eye of three hundred people applauding the ruin of her family, looked down into her cupped and empty hands. On the master tape, if you enlarge it, if you slow it the way the trial did, poster-size, you can see the old lips moving. The state's lip-reader put it in evidence, State's 141, and it hung in the sentencing brief and it hangs here, at the end of my part of this, which ends where hers finally began:

*"Drink your water. It's getting cold out."*

And the second thing. In the last clean frame before the doors — kettled by troopers, wrists chained, walking his unhurried walk out of the biggest room he would ever get — Tobias Usher turned his head, once, and swept the studio.

Left to right. Unhurried. The old census, one last time: counting who believed. Counting who didn't.

I watched the count come up short, and I watched him understand it — one flicker, one, in the flat interested place, and I am the only living witness to it and it is the closest thing to a confession that man will ever make — and then the doors took him.

He is counting still, in a cell upstate. I am counting still, in this book. One hundred and forty-one, and two I was late for, and the number does not balance and it never will.

The tip line stayed up all that night. At 3:12 a.m. it took a call from a pay phone — a pay phone, in 2019; the caller had learned young to keep his coins where the eyes couldn't count them — outside a diner in Ohio. The transcript runs eight words. Sofia, who had quit, drove in anyway at four in the morning to hand-carry it to the parish, because some machines get one last shift out of everybody:

*"This is Ethan. Tell Robbie the fence held."*
