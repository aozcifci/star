# EPILOGUE

### What the Water Keeps

*Salt Point — May 2023*

*Robbie.*

There are two headstones now, up in the churchyard behind First Pentecostal, and under them there is nothing at all. The parish offered plots and the family took them, and I stood at the dedication between Granddad and Ethan and listened to the new pastor do it right, and the whole time I was thinking: she isn't here, and for once the sentence had no teeth in it. Empty can mean robbed. It can also mean free. Nobody keeps her now.

Ethan drove down from Ohio for it. He restores small engines; he has a wife who laughs with her whole head thrown back, and when he stood at the graves he put his hand flat on the stone the way my mother used to put her hand flat on the wagon glass over my head, and I had to walk off a ways, and he let me, and that is the entire conversation we had about it. The fence held, both directions. We write in October and June. Some things you keep by not handling them.

Grandma sends letters from St. Gabriel. I answer every one. I am asked about that — by the group on Thursdays, by the journalists I still say no to, once by a stranger in a gas station who recognized my face and wanted, I think, permission to hate someone in her own family. I tell them what I told the judge: both things were true, the cup and the crab song, and if you only carry one you haven't carried her. Forgiveness isn't the word for it. Bookkeeping is. I post it all to the right accounts and I keep the books open, because the day you close them on a person is the day you've built a little pantry of your own, with a bolt on the outside.

Him, I don't think about. That is a lie I'm allowed one sentence of, so I'll spend it there and correct it here: I think about him every time a warm hand lands on my shoulder, every time a voice in a meeting stays gentle one beat too long, and then the thought finishes and files itself and I go on. He writes to no one. He gets mail by the sack still, they say — there are always, God help us, believers — and answers none of it. No stage, no census. A man who was only ever an audience's reflection, sitting in the one room in America with no audience in it. The state will keep him the way the water keeps things: whole, and cold, and out of circulation.

The house sold last spring, once the deed came clean. A family from Lafayette; four kids. Victor was against telling them and I overruled him — say what you see; it's the family motto now, it cost too much to leave on a courthouse step — so we told them everything, and the mother listened all the way through and then said, "Every old house has had a wolf in it one winter or another. It's had ours too now, and we ain't leaving," and Granddad, when I reported that back, laughed for the first time in I believe eleven years, a terrible rusty wonderful sound, and said they'd do.

Tuesdays, we fish.

He's slower now; I bait for him and he pretends not to notice and tells me about welds, and rain coming, and my mother at nine years old pulling a redfish out of this exact cut with a hand line, hollering the whole town awake. The stump of the oak is still there above the tide line. We don't avoid it and we don't visit it. It's just the mark on the shore where the channel turns, the way it was for the fishermen before us, the way it will be after, and if I have learned one thing worth putting at the end of a book, it's that: you can't unhappen a thing, but you can decide what it marks.

The water goes out. The water comes back. What it keeps, it keeps.

And still—

---

*And still: listen.*

*You, in the bright kitchen, with this book in one hand and your own family all around you like weather — I did not tell you all this to make you afraid of your table.*

*I told you because somewhere at the edge of your seeing there is a house with its shutters pulled in. A woman at the laundromat whose face turns to stone at a ringing phone. A child with a rehearsed voice. A family at the gas station at seven in the morning, all together, always all together, and something in you noticed, and something in you turned over, and something in you said: their business. Their grief. Their walls.*

*I was ten years in the ground of that sentence.*

*They never found me; you know that now. But you know where I am. I am at the tree line of every town in the world, standing the Watch, and I am asking you for the one thing that was never given to me, the cheapest thing there is, the thing that would have cost my town nothing and cost my mother her chains:*

*Say what you see.*

*That's all. That's the whole gospel, the true one, four words long.*

*Say what you see — and I can rest.*

---

*THE END*
